Key Facts Statement (KFS) for MSME Loans: What Every Borrower Should Check Before Signing

The interest rate is not the only cost of a business loan. Learn how to read your KFS, compare the Annual Percentage Rate (APR), and understand charges and instalments before accepting a term loan.

MSME Knowledge Hub · Regulatory references reviewed 23 November 2026 · 10-minute read

Suppose a small manufacturing business wants to buy a new machine. Two lenders offer a loan at the same stated interest rate, but one deducts more charges upfront. The loans may not cost the same in practice. The Key Facts Statement (KFS) helps the borrower see the important terms of a loan in a standard, readable form before signing the agreement.

This guide explains the KFS from the perspective of a first-time MSME borrower. It also gives an illustrative example that shows why the Annual Percentage Rate (APR) can be higher than the quoted interest rate.

Scope matters: The RBI's harmonised KFS framework applies to retail and MSME term loans of regulated lenders. It should not be read as an automatic rule for every cash-credit (CC), overdraft (OD), bank guarantee or letter-of-credit facility. Separate product-specific disclosure rules may apply.

1. What is a Key Facts Statement?

A KFS is a concise summary of important facts about a loan agreement. It is intended to make loan terms easier to understand and to help borrowers compare offers from different lenders. The RBI's circular dated 15 April 2024 established harmonised instructions for KFS disclosure on retail and MSME term loan products.

The lender must provide the KFS before the borrower executes the loan contract, in a language understood by the borrower, explain its contents and obtain an acknowledgement of understanding. It is not a substitute for the full loan agreement: borrowers should read the other documents too.

2. Which MSME loans are covered?

The RBI directions apply to new retail and MSME term loans sanctioned on or after 1 October 2024, including fresh term loans sanctioned to existing customers, by regulated entities such as banks and NBFCs within the circular's scope. A business purchasing machinery with a repayable term loan is a typical example.

An MSME may also have a cash-credit limit for inventory and receivables, or non-fund-based BG/LC facilities. Those facilities are not automatically covered by this particular harmonised term-loan KFS requirement merely because the borrower is an MSME. Always request the applicable written terms and fee disclosures for each facility.

3. What should a borrower find in the KFS?

The standardised form includes key loan terms and cost details. Read it alongside the attached APR calculation sheet and amortisation or repayment schedule. Look particularly for:

  • Loan amount and loan term: how much is sanctioned and for how long.
  • Interest rate and type: fixed, floating or hybrid, and relevant reset terms.
  • Repayment: instalment amount, number of instalments, frequency and commencement date.
  • Fees and charges: processing, documentation and relevant third-party costs.
  • APR: a measure of annualised credit cost that includes interest and applicable charges.
  • Other important contractual terms: items such as penal charges, if applicable, and relevant grievance-redressal details.

The KFS carries a unique proposal number. Under the directions it must remain valid for at least three working days where the loan term is seven days or more, and one working day where the loan term is under seven days. That is time for the borrower to consider the disclosed terms; it is not necessarily a promise that disbursement will occur during that period.

4. Interest rate versus Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

A quoted interest rate describes the interest calculation on the loan. APR is intended to express the overall annualised cost of credit, including the interest and applicable charges. Two loans with identical quoted rates may therefore have different APRs because of their fees, repayment patterns or timing of charges.

The RBI directions require the KFS to be accompanied by an APR computation sheet. Charges recovered by the lender on behalf of third-party providers, such as certain insurance or legal charges, must be included in APR and separately disclosed when the lender collects them. The actual treatment depends on the nature and timing of the charges. APR is not calculated correctly by simply adding the fee percentage to the stated interest rate.

5. Worked example: a ₹10 lakh machinery loan

Consider a hypothetical small unit buying a packaging machine. For illustration, assume that it takes a ₹10,00,000 term loan carrying 10% annual interest on a reducing balance, repayable in 36 equal monthly instalments. Assume the lender collects total one-time charges of ₹15,000 upfront at disbursement and that these are all included in APR. To keep the example straightforward, there are no later fees or rate changes.

Sanctioned loan₹10,00,000
Stated annual interest rate10% (monthly reducing balance)
Repayment period36 monthly EMIs
Monthly instalmentApproximately ₹32,267.19
Upfront processing and other eligible charges₹15,000
Net funds received after upfront deduction₹9,85,000
Total of 36 instalmentsApproximately ₹11,61,618.74
Total interest within those instalmentsApproximately ₹1,61,618.74
Illustrative effective annualised cost (IRR-based APR)Approximately 11.62%

Although the repayment schedule is computed on ₹10 lakh at a stated 10% interest rate, the business receives only ₹9.85 lakh after upfront fees. It then repays the scheduled EMIs on the full sanctioned loan. An internal-rate-of-return calculation based on the net amount received and the monthly payment cash flows produces an effective annualised cost of approximately 11.62%.

Practical point: The illustration assumes all ₹15,000 is deducted at the start and ignores tax and other charges. Changes in the fee payment dates, disbursement dates, interest resets or repayment schedule will change the APR. A lender's actual KFS calculation is authoritative for its offered product; this worked example is for learning only.

6. How to read the repayment schedule

An EMI usually contains both interest and principal. At the start of a reducing-balance term loan, a larger share of an EMI goes towards interest; as the outstanding principal falls, more of the same EMI goes towards principal. The lender should provide the amortisation schedule with the KFS so borrowers can see the expected progression.

For a business, the key question is not only whether the instalment looks affordable in a good sales month, but whether operating cash flow can cover it throughout the year. If revenues are seasonal, check whether a suitable repayment structure has been negotiated and properly documented. Our EMI, PED and negotiated repayment guide explains these alternatives, and the Business Loan EMI Calculator can help you estimate repayments.

7. Charges, consent and other protections

Under the RBI KFS framework, a regulated lender cannot add a fee or charge not mentioned in the KFS during the loan term without the borrower's explicit consent. Where the lender recovers amounts on behalf of third-party providers, it must separately disclose the applicable charges and provide receipts or related documents within a reasonable time.

Do not confuse a KFS with a guarantee of loan approval, or treat APR as the only consideration. Security requirements, business cash-flow suitability, rate-reset conditions, early repayment terms and the lender's service arrangements are also important. Review the sanction letter and complete loan agreement for the specific proposal.

8. Borrower's checklist before signing

Use this short list when reviewing your lender's offer. Keep the KFS and its annexures with your business loan records.

  • Does the KFS show the correct borrower name, loan amount and tenor?
  • Do I understand whether the interest rate is fixed or floating?
  • Have I compared the APR, not only the advertised interest rate?
  • Are processing charges and eligible third-party charges clearly disclosed?
  • Have I checked the EMI or other repayment schedule against expected cash flows?
  • Do I understand the security, margin, relevant prepayment and penal-charge terms?
  • Have I read the proposal validity period and obtained copies of the KFS and annexures?
  • Have I asked the lender to explain any term that is unclear?

9. Common questions

Is a KFS required for every MSME cash-credit account?

Not under the harmonised April 2024 KFS circular solely because it is an MSME facility. That circular specifically addresses retail and MSME term loan products. Other disclosure obligations can still apply.

What if the APR is higher than the stated interest rate?

That may reflect disclosed fees and the timing of cash flows. Ask the lender for the APR computation sheet and a breakdown of charges before comparing offers.

Can the lender levy a fee that is missing from the KFS?

Under the applicable RBI KFS directions, a fee or charge omitted from the KFS cannot be charged during the loan term without the borrower's explicit consent.

Does the KFS replace the sanction letter and loan agreement?

No. It summarises important facts and is included as a summary box in the loan agreement. Read the full contractual documents as well.

Can I compare two banks using APR alone?

APR is a helpful comparison measure, particularly for loans with comparable repayment structures. Also compare the repayment calendar, interest resets, collateral and other terms that affect the business.
