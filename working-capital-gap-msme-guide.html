Working Capital Gap for MSMEs: Calculation and Bank Appraisal

Learn the difference between current assets, current liabilities, working capital gap and bank finance.

Updated October 2026 · Independent educational information

Working capital gap measures the portion of operating current assets not funded by operating current liabilities. It is a planning concept; the bank's permissible finance can differ under the applicable assessment method.

Basic calculation

Working capital gap = operating current assets − operating current liabilities. For example, inventory ₹30 lakh plus receivables ₹25 lakh less trade creditors ₹15 lakh gives a gap of ₹40 lakh. This is not automatically the eligible cash-credit limit.

What to exclude or review

Examine non-operating assets, overdue receivables, slow-moving stock, related-party balances, advances and other items that may not be accepted for drawing power. A projected balance sheet and operating cycle should support the estimates.

Borrower contribution and bank finance

Assessment depends on the bank's current policies, the facility, turnover, operating cycle, borrower margin and regulatory framework. Do not apply a universal fixed percentage to every business.

Monitoring

Compare projected and actual inventory, collections and trade creditors each month. Review drawing power and stock statements separately from initial credit assessment.

Turning the operating cycle into a funding plan

Method and assumptions

Start with inventory, trade receivables and necessary cash, then deduct trade creditors and other operating current liabilities. Distinguish operating current liabilities from bank borrowings; assess borrower contribution separately under the lender’s method.

Illustrative Indian MSME case

An enterprise estimates eligible stock of ₹70 lakh, eligible receivables of ₹50 lakh and other operating current assets of ₹10 lakh, against trade creditors and accruals of ₹45 lakh. The indicative operating gap is ₹85 lakh before promoter margin, ineligible assets and sanction-specific adjustments.

Practical implementation

Prepare a month-by-month comparison of stock levels, debtor ageing and supplier credit. Test a slower collection cycle and check whether old receivables or obsolete inventory need exclusion.

Documents to keep ready

Maintain recent financial statements, sales and purchase ledgers, debtor and creditor ageing, stock reports, bank account statements and repayment schedules. The information required will vary with the product and lender.

Important limitations

Illustrative calculations are not credit decisions. The lender may adjust eligible assets, cash-flow definitions, margins, security values and repayment assumptions. Verify current terms before applying.

Worked example: why the gap is not the sanctioned cash-credit limit

Imagine a small trading business with stock of ₹30 lakh, customer receivables of ₹25 lakh and supplier credit of ₹15 lakh. Its operating working capital gap is ₹40 lakh (₹30 lakh + ₹25 lakh − ₹15 lakh). If a lender illustratively requires the business to contribute 25% of that gap, the contribution is ₹10 lakh and the balance is ₹30 lakh. This is only a planning illustration: an actual bank may use a different assessment method, apply other margins, or exclude old receivables and slow-moving stock.

What changes when customers pay late?

If receivables rise from ₹25 lakh to ₹35 lakh while stock and supplier credit stay unchanged, the gap increases to ₹50 lakh. The extra ₹10 lakh is money tied up in customer credit, not additional profit. Before seeking a larger limit, the entrepreneur should investigate collection delays, negotiate supplier terms and review stock turnover.

Three questions to ask before approaching a bank

  1. How much of the inventory is saleable and how much is old or obsolete?
  2. What portion of receivables is overdue, disputed or from related parties?
  3. Can the business provide its own contribution without borrowing informally at an unsustainable cost?
