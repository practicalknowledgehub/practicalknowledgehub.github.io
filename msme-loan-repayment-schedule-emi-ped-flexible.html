MSME Loan Repayment Schedule: EMI vs PED vs Flexible Repayment

Understand EMI, equal principal instalments and negotiated repayment with practical business cash-flow examples.

Updated October 2026 · Independent educational information

A repayment schedule tells you when principal and interest fall due. The best schedule is not always the one with the smallest initial payment: it should fit the cash-flow pattern of the business while controlling total borrowing cost.

EMI: equal monthly instalment

Under a standard EMI, the total monthly instalment remains constant while the interest share declines and principal share increases. For a ₹12 lakh loan at an illustrative fixed 12% annual interest over 36 months, the EMI is approximately ₹39,857. Interest is calculated on the reducing balance.

PED: principal equally distributed

Under PED, the same principal amount is repaid each month, plus interest on the outstanding balance. On ₹12 lakh over 36 months, monthly principal is ₹33,333.33. At 12% per annum, the first month's interest is ₹12,000, making the first payment ₹45,333.33; later payments decline. Total interest is usually lower than an EMI with the same principal, rate and term, but early instalments are higher.

Negotiated or structured repayment

Some businesses have seasonal sales or long project gestation periods. A lender may assess a schedule with lower early principal and larger later repayments, subject to projected cash generation and its credit policy. Interest still accrues on the outstanding principal. A back-loaded structure can increase total interest and refinancing risk.

Which repayment option should an MSME choose?

EMI can suit predictable monthly operating cash flows. PED can suit borrowers able to manage higher initial outflows while reducing principal faster. Negotiated repayment can suit seasonal businesses and projects with uneven cash generation, subject to lender approval. Compare total interest and stress-test repayment capacity before choosing.

Before agreeing to a schedule

  • Match repayments with monthly or quarterly cash-flow forecasts, not only projected profits.
  • Stress-test sales declines, slower debtor collection and rising floating interest rates.
  • Check moratorium interest, balloon payments, covenants and prepayment conditions.
  • Keep working-capital needs separate from long-term repayment commitments.
