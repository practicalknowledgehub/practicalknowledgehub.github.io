How to Calculate Working Capital Requirements for an MSME Business in India

Published 14 October 2026 • Educational article • Approx. 8-minute read

A profitable business can still face a cash shortage. The reason is simple: money is tied up in stock and customer payments while suppliers, wages and operating expenses must be paid. Working capital assessment helps estimate how much funding a business needs to run its normal operations.

What you will learn: the difference between gross and net working capital, how to estimate the operating cycle, a worked example in rupees, and why a bank's sanctioned cash credit limit may differ from the calculated working capital gap.

1. What is working capital?

Gross working capital usually refers to total current assets, such as inventory, trade receivables and cash. Net working capital (NWC) is current assets minus current liabilities.

Net Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

For credit assessment, banks may separately analyse the working capital gap: operating current assets less eligible spontaneous current liabilities, such as trade creditors. This is not necessarily the same as the accounting NWC shown on the balance sheet.

2. Understand the operating cycle

The operating cycle tracks the time between paying for materials and collecting money from customers. In a manufacturing business, the cycle can include raw material storage, production, finished goods holding and credit sales.

Cash Conversion Cycle ≈ Inventory Days + Receivable Days − Payable Days

Inventory days and payable days are commonly based on cost of goods sold or purchases, while receivable days are commonly based on credit sales. Use consistent assumptions and suitable averages when preparing a detailed assessment.

3. Worked example: a small manufacturing MSME

Consider a fictional manufacturing unit with the following annual figures. The example is deliberately simplified to explain the method; it is not a bank sanction calculation.

Business informationIllustrative amount
Annual sales (assumed entirely on credit)₹120 lakh
Annual cost of goods sold₹90 lakh
Inventory holding period60 days
Customer collection period45 days
Supplier credit period30 days

Step A: Estimate inventory requirement

Inventory = ₹90 lakh × 60 ÷ 365 = ₹14.79 lakh

Step B: Estimate trade receivables

Receivables = ₹120 lakh × 45 ÷ 365 = ₹14.79 lakh

Step C: Estimate supplier credit

Trade Payables = ₹90 lakh × 30 ÷ 365 = ₹7.40 lakh

Step D: Calculate the simplified working capital gap

Component₹ lakh
Inventory14.79
Trade receivables14.79
Total estimated operating current assets29.58
Less: Trade payables(7.40)
Simplified working capital gap22.18

The cash conversion cycle is approximately 60 + 45 − 30 = 75 days. The ₹22.18 lakh gap is a planning estimate before adding other current assets, expenses, liabilities, margins and bank-specific adjustments.

Important: A ₹22.18 lakh working capital gap does not mean the bank will automatically sanction ₹22.18 lakh. The business may need to bring its own margin; the lender will assess eligibility, projections, actual operations, existing liabilities, collateral or guarantee arrangements where applicable, and its applicable credit policy.

4. How do banks assess the cash credit limit?

Banks commonly examine historical and projected sales, production capacity, inventory records, debtor ageing, creditor terms, existing borrowings and cash flows. Depending on the product and applicable rules, assessment may use a turnover-linked approach, an operating-cycle or working-capital-gap approach, or a detailed cash-flow analysis.

Under a turnover-linked illustration, a working capital requirement might be estimated as a percentage of projected sales, with a portion funded by the borrower. Such percentages are not universal sanction rules; their use depends on the lender's current policy and the specific loan product.

5. How much margin must the borrower contribute?

Suppose a lender, solely for illustration, expects the borrower to fund 25% of the simplified ₹22.18 lakh gap. The estimated contribution would be ₹5.55 lakh, and the remaining ₹16.64 lakh could be considered for bank funding before eligibility and policy adjustments. Actual margin treatment may instead be based on prescribed components of current assets or other assessment conventions.

Illustrative borrower contribution: ₹22.18 lakh × 25% ≈ ₹5.55 lakh

6. Documents that support a realistic assessment

  • Audited or available financial statements and current provisional figures.
  • GST returns, sales records and bank account statements.
  • Stock statements, inventory ageing and stock valuation method.
  • Customer-wise receivable ageing, including overdue and disputed bills.
  • Supplier-wise payable ageing and normal credit terms.
  • Monthly sales and purchase projections supported by orders or capacity.
  • Details of existing limits, repayment obligations and promoter contribution.

7. Five common errors in working capital projections

  1. Inflated sales: projecting turnover without sufficient orders or operating capacity.
  2. Unrealistic debtor days: ignoring persistent collection delays or long-overdue invoices.
  3. Excess stock assumptions: failing to distinguish normal inventory from obsolete or slow-moving stock.
  4. Double counting expenses: mixing inventory values, operating costs and liabilities inconsistently.
  5. Confusing the gap with the loan: overlooking the borrower's contribution and the bank's separate eligibility assessment.

8. Practical ways to reduce the funding gap

Businesses can improve collections, negotiate reasonable supplier terms, plan purchases against production, monitor slow-moving inventory and prepare monthly cash-flow forecasts. A shorter cash conversion cycle can reduce the funding needed for the same level of sales, although every operational change involves trade-offs.

Frequently asked questions

Is working capital the same as a cash credit limit?

No. Working capital is a financial concept; a cash credit limit is a bank facility. The approved limit depends on assessment, eligibility and sanction conditions.

Does a higher current ratio always mean better working capital management?

No. A high ratio can reflect excess or obsolete inventory and uncollected receivables. Asset quality and the operating cycle matter alongside the ratio.

Can service businesses require working capital?

Yes. Service firms may need funds for salaries, contract execution and receivables even if they carry little inventory.

Can a business with profits still face a cash shortage?

Yes. Profit is measured on an accrual basis, while cash can be tied up in inventory and unpaid invoices.
