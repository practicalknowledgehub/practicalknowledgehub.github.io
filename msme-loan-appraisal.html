How Banks Assess MSME Business Loan Proposals in India

A step-by-step explanation of what lenders examine before sanctioning a business loan, including an illustrative working capital calculation and a repayment-capacity example.

When a micro, small or medium enterprise approaches a bank for finance, the bank does more than check the availability of collateral. It assesses the business, the purpose of borrowing, the promoter's contribution, financial performance, projected cash flows and the risks involved. The exact approach depends on the bank, loan product, amount and applicable policies.

Important: The figures below are fictional examples for learning, not a bank's sanction formula. Credit decisions depend on the lender's current policy, verified information and the borrower's circumstances.

1. Understand the borrower and the business

The appraisal begins with understanding who is borrowing and how the enterprise operates. A lender may examine:

  • Promoters' experience, ownership and management arrangements.
  • Business activity, products, customers, suppliers and competition.
  • Operating history, banking conduct and existing borrowing obligations.
  • Registration, statutory compliance and relevant licences.
  • Purpose of the proposed facility and the amount requested.

A convincing proposal explains why funds are required and how the borrowing will support business operations or growth.

2. Assess financial performance and financial position

Banks generally examine financial statements and related information to understand profitability, liquidity, leverage and the quality of assets. Common indicators include:

IndicatorWhat it helps assess
Sales and operating marginBusiness scale, earnings trends and sustainability
Current ratioShort-term liquidity, alongside the quality of current assets
Debt-to-equity ratioLeverage and dependence on borrowed funds
Receivable and inventory daysFunds tied up in the operating cycle
Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)Cash available to meet scheduled debt payments

Ratios are not interpreted in isolation. For example, a satisfactory current ratio may be less reassuring if a large portion of receivables is overdue or inventory is slow-moving.

3. Evaluate working capital requirements

Working capital supports everyday operations, including purchasing raw materials, holding stock and extending credit to customers. One useful starting point is the operating cycle and the level of current assets and spontaneous current liabilities.

Illustrative working capital example

Suppose a small manufacturing enterprise estimates the following average operating balances:

ItemAmount
Raw materials and other inventory₹30 lakh
Trade receivables₹40 lakh
Other operating current assets₹5 lakh
Total operating current assets₹75 lakh
Trade creditors and other eligible operating current liabilities₹20 lakh
Illustrative net operating working capital requirement₹55 lakh

Calculation: ₹75 lakh − ₹20 lakh = ₹55 lakh.

This ₹55 lakh is an illustrative funding requirement before considering the business's own contribution, available internal resources, eligibility of assets, existing facilities and the bank's applicable assessment method. It is not automatically the amount of cash credit the bank will sanction.

The lender will also test whether projected sales, stock levels and customer credit periods are reasonable. For instance, slower collection from customers can increase financing needs even when sales remain unchanged.

4. Examine repayment capacity for term loans

A term loan is generally repaid from cash generated by the business over time. Lenders may review projected cash flow, the proposed repayment schedule and the DSCR.

Illustrative DSCR example

Item (annual)Amount
Cash available for scheduled debt service, after relevant adjustments₹24 lakh
Scheduled loan principal repayments₹12 lakh
Interest included in debt service₹6 lakh
Total scheduled debt service₹18 lakh

Illustrative DSCR: ₹24 lakh ÷ ₹18 lakh = 1.33 times.

This means the assumed cash available for debt service is approximately 1.33 times the assumed scheduled payments. It does not by itself establish loan eligibility: definitions, acceptable levels and adjustments vary by lender and facility. Banks may also examine year-wise DSCR, stressed projections and existing obligations.

5. Test the assumptions behind projections

Projected sales and profits should be supported by practical evidence such as past trends, confirmed orders, capacity utilisation, market demand, input costs and collection patterns. A bank may question projections showing sharp sales growth without corresponding investment, capacity or working capital.

A useful proposal clearly separates historical results, management assumptions and projected outcomes.

6. Review security, contribution and risk

Depending on the loan product and applicable rules, a lender may examine the promoter's contribution, primary security, collateral where applicable, guarantees, insurance, existing charges and the enforceability of documents. Collateral alone does not replace an assessment of repayment ability.

Borrowers should also check whether relevant government-supported schemes or guarantee arrangements may apply, rather than assuming every MSME facility has identical collateral requirements.

7. Check documents and conduct due diligence

Documentation depends on the constitution of the business and the facility requested. Banks may seek identity and business records, financial statements, GST and income-tax information where applicable, bank statements, debt details, project reports, quotations, projections and security documents.

Inconsistencies between statements, loan applications and supporting schedules can slow the process. Maintaining a clear explanation of major changes in sales, margins, receivables and borrowings is often valuable.

8. Put the proposal together

A well-organised MSME loan proposal typically answers five practical questions:

  1. Who? Who owns and manages the enterprise?
  2. Why? Why is the finance needed now?
  3. How much? How was the requirement calculated?
  4. How repaid? What cash flows support repayment?
  5. What risks? What could go wrong, and how will the business manage it?
Key takeaway: A bankable proposal is not simply a request for a loan amount. It is a documented explanation of business viability, financial need, repayment ability and risk. Good preparation improves clarity but never guarantees approval.

Frequently asked questions

Does every MSME loan require collateral?

No. Requirements depend on the lender, facility, amount, applicable regulations and any eligible guarantee arrangements. Borrowers should confirm the conditions for the specific product.

Is a profitable business automatically eligible for a loan?

No. Banks also consider cash flow, existing liabilities, credit conduct, business risk, documentation and the proposed facility's terms.

Is working capital requirement the same as sanctioned cash credit limit?

No. Working capital need is one input. The sanctioned limit depends on the bank's assessment method, margin requirements, eligible current assets, existing finance and other considerations.

What DSCR is acceptable for an MSME loan?

There is no single universal threshold applicable to every bank and loan. Acceptable levels and calculation methods depend on the lender's policy and the risk profile of the proposal.
